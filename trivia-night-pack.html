How to play (30–60 seconds)

  • Split players into teams of 2–6. Hand each team one answer sheet page (they can also use scrap paper).
  • The host reads each round’s six questions aloud, slowly, twice. Teams write answers.
  • One point per correct answer. Host reads the printed answers between rounds: teams pass sheets to neighbors and score them.
  • Most points after eight rounds wins. If it’s close, use the tiebreakers: nearest guess wins, no going over.

Hosting Tips: A Great Night with No Screens Needed

This pack is built for libraries, classrooms, senior centers, and living rooms. Everything runs on paper and one confident voice. This page is for the host — consider not photocopying it for teams.

Before the night

  • Print smart: one full pack for the host, plus one Answer Sheets page per expected team (and a few spare). A copier friendly black-and-white print works fine.
  • Pencils over pens. Somebody will want to change an answer. Everybody always does.
  • Set the tone early: say out loud that this night is about fun and curiosity, not homework. Wrong guesses get chuckles, not sighs.
  • Optional: a small prize (bookmarks, candy, bragging rights on a paper certificate) works wonders for libraries and classrooms.

While running rounds

  • Read each question twice and then pause. Teams grumble “wait” far less when they know the second read is coming.
  • Stay in order. Announce the round name before reading; it helps teams settle and clue hunters feel clever early.
  • Grade between rounds, not at the end. Pass sheets right, read answers aloud, collect sheets back, repeat. It keeps energy up and disputes down.
  • Accept close answers. If a team writes “Turing” instead of “Alan Turing,” give the point. You are the referee and the vibe-keeper.
  • Handle disputes with a rule: host decides, then everybody says “moving on” together. Playful, not lawyerly.

Notes for libraries and classrooms

  • Accessibility: read questions clearly and at a slower pace; large-print answer sheets can be made by enlarging at the copier (the pack prints fine at 120%).
  • Mixed ages work: rounds mix history, everyday tech, and pop culture so grandparents and teenagers can trade wins.
  • No devices rule (optional): decide once whether phones are allowed, and say it at the start. This pack is great fully unplugged.
  • Follow-up hooks: round answers connect to beginner-friendly AI explainers. A line like “if you enjoyed Round 3, the library’s beginner AI guides start even simpler” is a nice closer.

Scoring at a glance

  • 8 rounds × 6 questions × 1 point = 48 possible points
  • Tie at 48 or anywhere else? Tiebreakers are nearest-guess: closest number without going over wins (like The Price Is Right, but for 1956).
  • Teams can score themselves between rounds — it keeps one person out of math jail for the whole evening.

Round 1: Influential Moments

The history round. Six landmark moments in AI, from a thoughtful mathematician to a chatbot that broke the internet.

  1. In 1950, which British mathematician proposed the “imitation game” as a way to ask whether machines can think? Answer: Alan Turing — the idea became known as the Turing Test.
  2. The term “artificial intelligence” was coined at a famous summer workshop in 1956. At which university did it happen? Answer: Dartmouth College — the 1956 Dartmouth workshop. Accept “Dartmouth.”
  3. In 1997, which IBM computer defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov? Answer: Deep Blue.
  4. A 2017 research paper titled “Attention Is All You Need” introduced what architecture, now the engine inside most modern chatbots? Answer: The Transformer.
  5. In 2016, which program beat Go grandmaster Lee Sedol, surprising almost everyone? Answer: AlphaGo (made by Google DeepMind).
  6. ChatGPT launched in which month of which year? Answer: November 2022.

Host note: for question 6, accept November of 2022 only — year alone gets the point; month and year gets applause.

Round 2: Common Myths — True or False?

Read each statement. Teams write “True” or “False.” After reading the answer, feel free to add the one-line reason.

  1. True or False: AI chatbots understand language the same way humans do. Answer: False — they predict likely words based on patterns in their training data. Clever, but not human understanding.
  2. True or False: When a chatbot sounds confident, its answer is more likely to be right. Answer: False — chatbots can be confidently wrong. Confidence and accuracy are separate dials.
  3. True or False: A chatbot learns from your conversation in real time and updates itself live as you chat. Answer: False — the model is trained beforehand. It doesn’t “learn” from tonight’s chat live.
  4. True or False: AI chatbots are conscious and have feelings. Answer: False — there is no evidence AI is conscious. A chatbot can write passionately about coffee without having tasted any.
  5. True or False: Every AI tool can see images and hear audio by default. Answer: False — many AI tools only handle text. Multimodal ones exist, but text-only is still common.
  6. True or False: A chatbot making something up — stating it as fact — has a name in the AI world. Answer: True — it’s called a “hallucination”: a confident, made-up answer.

Round 3: How It Works

Plain-English questions about what AI actually does under the hood. No engineering degree required, as promised.

  1. The instruction or question you type to an AI tool has a specific two-syllable name. What is it? Answer: A prompt.
  2. When an AI chatbot confidently gives a false or entirely invented answer, it’s said to be doing what? Answer: Hallucinating (the answer is called a hallucination).
  3. When your phone suggests your next word as you type a message, it’s using the same core trick as large chatbots. What is that trick? Answer: Predicting the next word.
  4. What do we call the examples a model is built from — the books, articles, and web pages it learned patterns from? Answer: Training data (the model is trained on it).
  5. The word “GPT” in ChatGPT is an abbreviation. Name any two of the three words it stands for. Answer: Generative Pre-trained Transformer — any two of the three earns a point.
  6. Many phones unlock just by looking at you. What kind of AI makes that possible? Answer: Face recognition (computer vision).

Round 4: Staying Safe

This round quietly teaches something useful. All six answers are habits that protect real people and real money.

  1. Highly realistic faked photos, videos, or voices are commonly called what? Answer: Deepfakes.
  2. Voice-cloning scammers can produce a passable copy of someone’s voice from roughly how much audio — minutes, or just a few seconds? Answer: A few seconds.
  3. Scam phone calls now often use cloned voices and fake urgency. What’s one safe habit to stop it cold? Answer: Hang up and call the person back on a number you know / verify through a separate channel. (Accept: set a family code word.)
  4. What kind of information should you never type into a public AI chatbot? Answer: Personal or sensitive information — passwords, Social Security numbers, bank or medical details. (Accept sensible examples.)
  5. Fake emails and texts that try to trick you into clicking links or handing over logins have an old name — what is it? Answer: Phishing.
  6. When an AI offers to “verify” who you are by asking for personal details in chat, what should you do first? Answer: Be suspicious — verify through an official channel before sharing anything (or simply don’t share). (Accept: refuse/close the chat.)

Host note: this round is intentionally “answers may vary” on 3 and 6. The takeaway sentence works every time: slow down, verify separately, share less.

Round 5: AI in Everyday Life

Spoiler: everyone in the room has used AI today. These questions prove it.

  1. Which typing feature quietly fixes your typos as you text? Answer: Autocorrect.
  2. “Recommended for you” rows on Netflix or YouTube come from what kind of AI? Answer: Recommendation AI (recommender algorithms).
  3. Your maps app reroutes you around traffic. What is it doing under the hood? Answer: Using AI/live prediction on real-time traffic data (accept: prediction).
  4. Which device listens for “Hey Alexa” or “Hey Google” in your kitchen? Answer: A smart speaker (voice assistant).
  5. What silently decides, before you even open your email, which messages go to spam? Answer: A spam filter (machine-learning-based).
  6. At self-checkout, what kind of AI identifies a yellow item between a lemon and an onion? Answer: Computer vision.

Real-world AI players. Match the maker to the machine and name the company.

Round 7: Pop Culture AI

Movies and shows shaped how the world imagines AI — usually as a villain with excellent posture.

  1. “I’m sorry, Dave. I’m afraid I can’t do that.” Which calm, red-eyed computer said it in the 1968 film 2001: A Space Odyssey? Answer: HAL 9000.
  2. Which 1999 movie imagines humanity living unknowingly inside a simulated world, offered a red pill to escape? Answer: The Matrix.
  3. Which gentle robot’s day job was compacting trash on an abandoned Earth, in a 2008 Pixar film? Answer: WALL·E.
  4. “I’ll be back.” Which 1984 film features a killer robot sent from the future? Answer: The Terminator.
  5. In Star Trek: The Next Generation, what’s the name of the beloved android officer? Answer: Data.
  6. In 2011, which IBM system won Jeopardy! against champions Ken Jennings and Brad Rutter? Answer: IBM Watson.

Round 8: The Mixed Bag

A pinch of everything to finish the night. Whoever leads after this round either earned it or got lucky — both count.

  1. What three-letter abbreviation stands for “Artificial Intelligence”? Answer: AI.
  2. “AGI” is shorthand in the AI world for a hypothetical AI that can do virtually any intellectual task a person can. What does AGI stand for? Answer: Artificial General Intelligence.
  3. What do we call a program you can have a conversation with by typing or talking? Answer: A chatbot. (Accept: conversational agent.)
  4. What does it mean for an AI model to be “open-source” or openly downloadable? Answer: Its files are shared publicly so anyone can download and run it themselves (often under a free license).
  5. Most big chatbots let you use them for free, with paid upgrades available. What is that payment model commonly called? Answer: Freemium (free tier + paid/premium subscription).
  6. Fittingly, the final question: before answering a trivia question, a smart team might double-check with a second source. What everyday skill is that an example of when AI is involved? Answer: Verifying AI answers (fact-checking / not trusting a single source). Accept any answer that says checking.

Tiebreakers

How to run them: each team writes a single number on the back of their answer sheet. Closest guess without going over wins the round. If everyone busts, the closest overall wins. Ask one at a time; stop when the tie breaks.

  1. The word “robot” first appeared in a 1920 play by Karel Čapek. In what year? (Teams write a year; nearest without going over wins.) Answer: 1920.
  2. The Dartmouth workshop that coined “artificial intelligence” happened in a single summer. What year was it? (Nearest without going over wins.) Answer: 1956.
  3. In the 1997 six-game rematch between IBM’s Deep Blue and Garry Kasparov, how many of the six games ended in a draw? (Nearest without going over wins.) Answer: 3.

Sudden-death backup: ask teams to guess the number of questions in this pack (48 regular, 3 tiebreakers). Nearest without going over wins. Or award the win to the team that can say “hallucination” with the most confidence. It’s on brand.
